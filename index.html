The problem

Guides of equal validity cleave at very different rates

  • Two measured guides from the dataset, same length and both structurally valid
  • Their cleavage efficiencies differ by a factor of five, with no visible cause
GRNA-3AAAAAACACAAGCAAGACCG TGG
0.248
GRNA-5AAAAAACCCGTAGATAGCCT CGG

Problem statement

Given only the 21-nucleotide sequence of a candidate guide, predict its cleavage efficiency, so that candidates can be ranked computationally rather than screened experimentally.

Three tiers of models

Each tier adds exactly one capability to the one below

Tier 1: tabular

  • 439 engineered sequence features
  • Ridge, XGBoost
  • LightGBM†, CatBoost†, Ensemble†

Tier 2: sequence only

  • Raw 21-mer, one-hot encoded
  • No feature engineering at all
  • LSTM, BiLSTM, CNN

Tier 3: sequence + biology

  • Adds ~100 biophysical descriptors
  • BiLSTM + bio
  • CNN + bio†

† models added beyond the paper's seven

09
03

Tier 1

Gradient-boosted trees on engineered features

  • 439 features per guide: positional nucleotide identity, k-mer counts, GC content, melting temperature
  • Ridge as a linear reference; XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost as boosted trees
  • An equal-weight ensemble of the three boosters, our strongest Tier-1 model

XGBoost reproduced the paper's published 0.8454 exactly, while training on 8.5% less data.

10
03

Tier 2

Sequence-only deep models

  • Input is the raw 21-mer, one-hot encoded, with no engineered features
  • CNN: convolutional filters detect local motifs independently of position
  • LSTM / BiLSTM: recurrent layers read the sequence in order, accumulating context

Learned representations beat hand-engineered features by +0.0081 over the best Tier-1 model.

11
03

Tier 3

Sequence combined with biophysical features

  • Adds ~100 biophysical descriptors to the Tier-2 input
  • Example bio features:
    • RNA secondary-structure free energy (ViennaRNA), how tightly the guide folds back on itself
    • Melting temperature of the guide and of its sub-regions
    • GC content and nucleotide composition

The same features gave the paper's RNN +0.0115, so the effect reproduces on a different backbone.

The metric

Spearman ρ, correlation of ranks rather than of values

  • Both lists are converted to ranks (1st, 2nd, 3rd), then correlated
  • A model is rewarded for ordering guides correctly, not for predicting exact rates
  • Unlike R², which penalises how far each prediction sits from the measured value, ρ ignores the distances entirely, so a model can rank perfectly while every number it outputs is wrong. Our R² is 0.71–0.80.

ρ = 1.00

predicted  0.10  0.50  0.90   ranks  1 2 3
measured   0.20  0.60  0.95   ranks  1 2 3

Every predicted value is wrong, yet the ranking is identical.

ρ = 0.50

predicted  0.59  0.21  0.96   ranks  2 1 3
measured   0.20  0.60  0.95   ranks  1 2 3

Values are nearly exact, but two guides are ranked the wrong way round.

Why we differ

Three methodological differences, each deliberate

  1. 01

    Training-set size

    They trained their tree models on 85/15 with no validation set; we hold out 76.5/8.5/15 throughout. Our XGBoost therefore saw less data than theirs did, and still reproduced their score exactly.

  2. 02

    Feature selection

    They narrowed a pool of 1,031 features down with Tree SHAP before training. We pass all 439 of ours in with no selection step at all.

  3. 03

    Hyper-parameter tuning

    They tuned each model separately by Bayesian optimisation. We deliberately froze a single schedule so the tier comparisons stay controlled, which accounts for the entire BiLSTM shortfall.

Limitations and future work

Limitations

  • Limited compute. One quarter on free Colab GPUs, so every run had to be chosen carefully.
  • A single split, not the paper's ten, which makes our error bar smaller than theirs in scope.
  • One nuclease. We ran WT-SpCas9 only; the two high-fidelity variants are untouched.
  • One baseline missing. We never built the paper's MLP.

Future work

  1. 01Build and train the other models: the paper's RNN and RNN + biofeatures, plus the Transformer, multi-task and stacked models already written in our codebase.
  2. 02Run a full tenfold evaluation so our error bar measures the same thing theirs does.
  3. 03Extend to eSpCas9(1.1) and SpCas9-HF1.

Summary

  • We reproduced a Nature paper end to end, from its published description alone, without ever opening the authors' source code.
  • Our best model reaches ρ = 0.8668 against their 0.8670 ± 0.0024, inside the range their own ten runs cover.
  • Our XGBoost reproduces their published 0.8454 to four decimal places, on less training data than they used.
  • Each tier improved on the one below it at a fixed training schedule, so the gains are attributable to the tier rather than to tuning.

One schedule

Identical hyper-parameters across every deep model

Fixed for all five deep models

epochs        = 60
batch         = 256
learning_rate = 0.001
rnn_layers    = 1
patience      = 10
  • LSTM → BiLSTM isolates bidirectionality
  • BiLSTM → BiLSTM+bio isolates the biological features
  • The paper instead tuned each model by Bayesian optimisation

Holding the schedule fixed makes each tier gap attributable, at the cost of absolute performance.
